## Literature Review

With **the growing** prevalence of AI in workplaces, managers commonly **confront three related** situations: organisations **that reject or delay** AI; organisations **that** provide digital tools that staff **barely** use; and employees who use AI privately **without organisational awareness or oversight**. These **situations map onto three familiar** positions on technological change**—pessimistic (Luddite), optimistic (technophile), and pragmatic—and together define a core managerial challenge: how to govern AI adoption so that technology, people and organisational control remain aligned.**

The first situation **reflects a pessimistic or** Luddite **stance, centred on** fears **of** job displacement, rising unemployment **and inequity**. Frey and Osborne (2017, p. 265) **famously estimated that a large share of** US occupations are highly susceptible to **computerisation**, **intensifying** concerns about wage **polarisation** and social injustice. **Such anxieties help explain why some organisations and workers resist AI. Classic change research remains relevant here.** Coch and French (1948) **showed that** participation in the design of **change reduces** resistance, while Dent and Goldberg (1999) argue that **people rarely resist “change” itself; they resist expected losses of** income, comfort, **status or** certainty, or they **doubt that** the change will work. **For managers, the implication is not simply to push AI harder, but to** address worker anxiety, protect vulnerable roles and consider **equity** once AI **begins to reshape tasks.**

By contrast, **technophile accounts emphasise adaptation over rejection.** Autor (2015, p. 26) argues that technological change has historically created **new forms of** work and that managers should focus on **managing transitions** rather than resisting technology. **From this view,** AI can **automate** routine data gathering and repetitive processes while **amplifying** the value of human **judgement.** Wilson and Daugherty (2018) **contend** that the **strongest** performance gains arise when humans and AI collaborate, each **reinforcing** the other’s strengths. **Optimistic theory therefore frames unused organisational AI less as a reason to abandon technology and more as a failure of redesign, training and role reconfiguration.**

**The second situation—tools provided but unused—shows that organisational acceptance of AI does not guarantee employee acceptance.** Davis’s (1989, p. 320) Technology Acceptance Model **(TAM) posits** that **intention to use a system depends mainly on** perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. **If staff see little benefit, or find tools cumbersome, they will ignore official platforms even when leadership endorses AI.** Managerial responses therefore include **simplifying interfaces, clarifying performance benefits, and providing** training that **makes usefulness visible in everyday tasks—not merely issuing devices or licences.**

**The third situation, private or “shadow” AI use, raises questions of control, transparency and accountability.** Kellogg et al. (2020, p. 366) argue that algorithmic systems **operate** as a new form of organisational control through mechanisms of direction, evaluation and discipline. Parent-Rocheleau and Parker (2022, p. 12) further show that algorithmic management reshapes job design—affecting autonomy, complexity and workload—and that transparency, fairness and **retained** human influence can moderate negative effects. **When employees adopt external AI tools outside formal systems, managers face a contested terrain: AI is no longer only a productivity aid, but also a surrogate for managerial oversight and a potential compliance risk.**

**A** pragmatic **middle** position **offers the most usable** guidance for **AI-embedded** workplaces. **It** recognises both risks and opportunities **and stresses cautious,** experimental and human-centred **practice.** Bankins **et al.** (2024, p. 177) show that outcomes of AI use depend heavily on how **human–AI** collaboration is designed and how worker attitudes are managed. Raisch and Krakowski (2021, p. 192) **frame** the core managerial task as deciding when AI should replace tasks and when it should **augment** human capabilities. Shrestha et al. (2019) **similarly** emphasise **redesigning** organisational decision-making structures so that human and algorithmic inputs are **integrated rather than left in tension.** **Collectively, these debates supply an evaluative lens for the photos that follow: each image can be read in terms of (1) resistance and feared loss, (2) acceptance via usefulness and ease of use, and (3) control, transparency and the replace-versus-augment choice facing managers.**
